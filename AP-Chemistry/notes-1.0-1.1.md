## Section 1.0: Particulate Diagrams

### Phases of Matter
- Particulate diagrams represent the phases of matter (solid, liquid, gas)
- Arrows on gas particles represent **direction of movement and velocity**
- Particles do NOT all have the same velocity

### Particulate Diagrams for Water (H₂O)
- Water is a **covalent compound** — can exist as solid, liquid, or gas
- In solid: molecules are locked in a rigid lattice
- In liquid: molecules are close but sliding past each other
- In gas: molecules are spread out and moving fast

### Metallic Solids and Alloys
- **Alloys** = mixtures of metals
- **Substitutional alloys** = one metal atom substituted for another of **similar size**
  - Example: Bronze (Copper + Tin)
- **Interstitial alloys** = smaller atoms inserted into holes of the metal lattice
  - Example: Steel (Iron + Carbon)

### Ionic Compounds
- Ionic compounds exist as **solids** at room temperature
- Positive and negative ions are **electrostatically attracted** to each other
- The arrangement of ions is called a **crystal lattice**

### Solvation (Dissolving Ionic Compounds in Water)
- **Solvation** = the process of dissolving an ionic solute in water
- During solvation:
  - **Positive ions (cations)** are surrounded by the **oxygen end** of water (oxygen = negative dipole, δ−)
  - **Negative ions (anions)** are surrounded by the **hydrogen end** of water (hydrogen = positive dipole, δ+)
- "Like dissolves like" — water is polar, so it dissolves ionic/polar substances

### Reading Particulate Diagrams of Solutions
- **Concentration** = number of particles in a given volume
- Flask with double the concentration has double the ions
- **MgCl₂** produces 2 Cl⁻ for every 1 Mg²⁺ → twice as many anions as cations
- **KCl** produces 1 K⁺ for every 1 Cl⁻ → equal cations and anions
- **Covalent solutes** (like HF) do NOT ionize in water — weak acids don't dissociate much
  - You can tell a solute is covalent/molecular if the molecules stay intact in the diagram

### Chemical vs Physical Changes (Particulate View)
- **Mixture** of iron and sulfur: both elements visible separately, no bonding
- **Iron(II) Sulfide (compound)**: atoms are now bonded in a fixed ratio — this is a chemical change
- Physical change = state changes (solid→liquid→gas), no new substance
- Chemical change = new substance formed (bonds broken/formed)

### Gas Behavior in Particulate Diagrams
- Arrows show direction and **relative speed** (longer arrow = faster particle)
- **(a)** Helium gas at 298 K in closed piston — baseline
- **(b)** Increase temperature at **constant volume** → particles move faster (longer arrows), same spacing
- **(c)** Increase temperature at **constant pressure** → volume increases, particles spread out
- **(d)** Increase external pressure at **constant temperature** → volume decreases, particles forced closer together
- Number of particles changes as volume changes (same sample size, different density)

## Section 1.1: Moles and Molar Mass

### The Mole
- The **mole (mol)** connects the amount of a substance to the number of particles
- 1 mole = **6.022 × 10²³** representative particles (Avogadro's Number, N_A)

### Representative Particles
Different substances have different representative particles:

| Substance type | Representative particle | Example |
|---------------|------------------------|---------|
| Monatomic element | Atom | C |
| Covalent compound | Molecule | C₁₂H₂₂O₁₁ (sugar) |
| Diatomic element | Molecule | N₂ |
| Ionic compound | Formula unit | LiCl |

- **Monatomic elements** → made of atoms
- **Covalent compounds** → made of molecules (atoms sharing valence electrons)
- **Diatomic elements** → made of molecules (H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂)
- **Ionic compounds** → made of formula units (electrons transferred, ions electrostatically attracted)

### Avogadro's Number and Conversion Factors
N_A = 6.022 × 10²³

**Converting particles → moles:**
```
             1 mol
particles × ———————— = moles
             6.022×10²³ particles
```

**Converting moles → particles:**
```
              6.022×10²³ particles
moles ×      ———————— = particles
              1 mol
```

### Molar Mass
- **Molar mass** = mass in grams of 1 mole of a substance (units: g/mol)
- Found using the **periodic table** (atomic mass = molar mass for elements)
- For compounds: sum the molar masses of all atoms

**Example: H₂O**
- H = 2 × 1.01 = 2.02
- O = 1 × 16.00 = 16.00
- Total = **18.02 g/mol**

**Converting grams → moles:**
```
           1 mol
grams × ———————— = moles
           molar mass (g)
```

**Converting moles → grams:**
```
           molar mass (g)
moles × ———————— = grams
           1 mol
```

### Molar Volume (at STP)
- **STP** = Standard Temperature and Pressure
  - Standard Temperature = 0°C
  - Standard Pressure = 1 atm
- At STP, 1 mole of any ideal gas = **22.4 L**

**Converting liters → moles (at STP):**
```
           1 mol
liters × ———————— = moles
           22.4 L
```

**Converting moles → liters (at STP):**
```
           22.4 L
moles × ———————— = liters
           1 mol
```

### Worked Example Problems

**Example 1: Mass from moles**
Calculate the mass of 1.5 mol H₂:
```
1.5 mol H₂ × (2.02 g H₂ / 1 mol H₂) = 3.03 g H₂
```

**Example 2: Volume from moles at STP**
Calculate the volume of 1.5 mol H₂ at STP:
```
1.5 mol H₂ × (22.4 L H₂ / 1 mol H₂) = 33.6 L H₂
```

**Example 3: Moles from molecules**
Determine moles in 1.8×10²³ molecules of H₂:
```
1.8×10²³ molecules H₂ × (1 mol H₂ / 6.02×10²³ molecules) = 0.30 mol H₂
```

**Example 4: Mass from volume at STP (two-step)**
Determine mass of 4.5 L CO₂ at STP:
```
4.5 L CO₂ × (1 mol CO₂ / 22.4 L CO₂) × (44.01 g CO₂ / 1 mol CO₂) = 8.8 g CO₂
```

**Example 5: Atoms from mass (two-step)**
How many atoms in 100.0 g Mg:
```
100.0 g Mg × (1 mol Mg / 24.31 g Mg) × (6.02×10²³ atoms / 1 mol Mg) = 2.4×10²⁴ atoms Mg
```
